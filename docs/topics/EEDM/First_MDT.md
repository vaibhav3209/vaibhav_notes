#### 3. What is Blue baby syndrome?
Ans.

Blue Baby Syndrome is a condition in infants in which the skin turns bluish (cyanosis) due to insufficient
oxygen in the blood. It is mainly caused by high nitrate levels in drinking water, which convert hemoglobin
into methemoglobin, reducing its ability to carry oxygen. This leads to breathing difficulties, weakness, and in
severe cases, can be life-threatening. The condition, also known as methemoglobinemia, can be prevented by
ensuring safe drinking water with nitrate concentrations below the permissible limit of 45 mg/L (IS 10500
standard).

#### 10. Infiltration Galleries

An infiltration gallery is a- horizontal or nearly horizontal tunnel which is constructed through water bearing
strata. It is sometimes referred to as horizontal well. The gallery is usually constructed of brick walls with slab
roof as shown in the figure.

The gallery obtains its water from water bearing strata by various porous drainpipes. These pipes are covered with gravel, pebble, etc. to prevent the entry of very fine material into the pipe. The gallery is laid at a slope and the water collected in the gallery is led to a sump from where it is pumped and supplicd to consumers after
proper treatment. The manholes are provided along the infiltration gallery for the purposes of cleaning and inspection. The infiltration galleries are useful as sources of water supply when ground water is available in sufficient quantity just below ground level or so. The galleries are usually constructed at depth of about 5 to 10 meters from the ground level.

Infiltration galleries and wells are important components of groundwater management systems, playing a crucial
role in meeting the demand for safe underground water.

- Infiltration galleries are underground structures designed to capture and store surface
water runoff or treated wastewater, allowing it to slowly percolate into the ground.
- They are typically constructed with perforated pipes or trenches filled with gravel, sand, or other porous
materials to facilitate water infiltration.
- Infiltration galleries help recharge aquifers by replenishing groundwater supplies, maintaining water
levels, and improving water quality through natural filtration.
- These structures are particularly useful in areas with limited surface water resources, aiding in drought
mitigation and sustaining water availability.


#### 11. Artesian Spring:
When ground water appears at the surface for any reason, springs are formed. They scrve as source of water supply for small towns, especially near hills or bases of hills. Some springs discharge hot water due to presence of sulphur and other minerals in their formations. These hot springs cannot be used to supply water for domestic purposes. But hot water is found to cure some of the human disorders. A good spring proves to be a sure source of water. But it is difficult to find a good spring for the purpose of water supply scheme.
Artesian spring is a source of ground water that is forced to the earth's surface by of pressure caused by
underground layers of rock surrounding the aquifer. It differs from a regular water well in that it doesn't
require a pump to get the water to the surface. The only requirement is gravity and the pressure it creates. For
this reason, Artesian springs occur at the base of a mountain or hill where the water can flow down into a well.

Artesian springs form when groundwater trapped under pressure within an aquifer naturally flows to the
surface. Water from rain or snow seeps into the ground in elevated areas, percolating through porous rock and
becoming confined between impermeable layers. As more water enters the aquifer, hydrostatic pressure builds
due to gravity and the trapped nature of the water. When this pressure becomes strong enough, and a fissure or
crack provides an outlet, the water is forced upward to the surface, creating an artesian spring. This natural
process requires no pumping, as the water flows freely under the pressure.

#### 13. Water Standards
Ans. 

Drinking water standards are guidelines set to ensure that water is safe for consumption, free from
contaminants that could harm human health. These standards vary across countries but generally include limits
on various physical, chemical, and biological parameters.
- Physical Standards: These focus on aspects like color, turbidity (cloudiness), odor, and taste. Water should
be clear and free of visible particles, unpleasant smells, and tastes that indicate contamination.
- Chemical Standards: These measure the presence of substances such as heavy metals (lead, arsenic),
pesticides, and minerals (fluoride, chlorine). Excessive levels of these chemicals can pose serious health risks,
such as cancer or kidney damage.
- Biological Standards: These address the presence of harmful microorganisms like bacteria, viruses, and
protozoa. High concentrations of pathogens can cause waterborne diseases like cholera and typhoid. Safe
drinking water should have no detectable pathogens.
- Microbiological Standards: Water should be free from harmful microorganisms such as Escherichia coli (Е.
coli) and coliform bacteria, which indicate fecal contamination.


#### 14. Water Supply Requirements 
Ans. 

Urban Water Supply: Urban systems must cater to large populations, requiring extensive infrastructure like
reservoirs, treatment plants, and pipelines. The water source must be reliable, often drawn from rivers, lakes,
or groundwater. Urban systems also need advanced treatment facilities to ensure water quality, removing
pollutants like industrial waste, chemicals, and pathogens.

Rural Water Supply: In rural areas, water sources are typically more decentralized, such as wells, boreholes,
or small rivers. Rural systems often rely on simpler infrastructure, but they face challenges in maintaining
reliable water quality due to limited access to advanced treatment technologies. Water sources may also be
more prone to contamination from agriculture or animal waste.


The estimation of water demand for a developing urban area is an important step in water supply engineering,
as it helps in designing systems that can meet both present and future needs.

1. Factors Affecting Water Requirement:
- Population size and growth: The demand increases with population; design periods of 15-30 years
are generally adopted.
- Per Capita Requirement (LPCD): According to IS 1172:1993, the water demand in Indian cities
varies from 100-150 liters per capita per day (LPCD) depending on living standards and service
levels.
- Climate: Hot and dry regions show higher water demand compared to cooler areas.
- Standard of living: Metropolitan cities with modern facilities need more water compared to small
towns.
- Industrial and commercial activities: Contribute about 15-20% of total demand in urban areas.
- Losses and wastage: Unaccounted-for water and leakage usually range between 15-20% of total
supply.

2. Estimation of Demand:
The total water demand is calculated in terms of:
- Average daily demand = Population LPCD
- Maximum daily demand ≈ 1.8 × Average demand
- Peak hourly demand ≈2.7 × Average demand
This ensures that the system can handle both normal and peak conditions.

3. Sources of Water to Meet Demand:
- Surface Water: Rivers, lakes, and reservoirs are the primary sources, though they require full
treatment (sedimentation, filtration, disinfection).
- Groundwater: Wells, tube wells, and springs provide relatively clean water but may contain hardness,
fluoride, or iron beyond limits prescribed in IS 10500:2012 (Drinking Water Standards).
- Rainwater Harvesting: Useful for groundwater recharge and reducing demand on conventional
sources.
- Other Sources: Desalination (in coastal areas) and reclaimed wastewater for non-potable use.

4. Importance in Environmental Engineering:
By estimating water demand and selecting suitable sources, engineers ensure safe, adequate, and sustainable
supply. This protects public health, supports industries and agriculture, and promotes environmental.

water and convey it to the treatment plant. The design depends on source conditions, water quality, and
topography.

Classification of Intakes

1. River Intakes
- Constructed on river banks with an intake well and pipe leading to a jack well/pump house.
- Provided with screens to prevent entry of debris, fish, and silt.
- Application: Suitable for cities/towns near perennial rivers.
Example: Water supply of Varanasi from River Ganga

2. Canal Intakes
- Built on canals diverted from rivers/reservoirs.
- Usually consist of a simple pipe intake with coarse and fine screens.
- Application: Used in irrigation-based states like Punjab, Haryana.
Example: Chandigarh draws water from Bhakra Canal.

3. Reservoir / Lake Intakes
- Intake towers are built inside lakes/reservoirs.
- Multi-level openings are provided to draw water at different depths depending on quality.
- Application: Suitable for big cities supplied from reservoirs.
Example: Bhatsa Reservoir supplying Mumbai, Upper Lake supplying Bhopal.

4. Tank Intakes
- Used in small ponds/tanks in rural areas.
- Simple pipe intake with screens connected to apumping unit.
- Application: Suitable for villages where irrigation tanks serve as sourcе.
Example: Traditional tanks in Tamil Nadu and Karnataka for rural water supply.

5. Dam Intakes
- Multi-level intakes built in dam reservoirs.
- Gates at different levels allow drawing of water with desired quality.
- Application: Urban water supply from large dams.
Example: Nagarjuna Sagar Dam supplying Hyderabad.

6. Sea (Coastal) Intakes
- Used for desalination plants in coastal areas.
Two types:

a) Open Channel Intake - draws directly from sea.

b) Infiltration Gallery Intake - draws filtered seawater through seabed.

- Application: Cities near the sea with no freshwater sources.
Example: Chennai desalination plants.

     - Must be agreeable and free from offensive smell.
    
     - Causes: Algae, organic pollution, or chlorination byproducts.


- Total Dissolved Solids (TDS):

#### BREAK POIINT Chlorination
Break point chlorination is a critical process in water treatment that involves the addition of chlorine until 
the point at which all the available impurities are oxidized
, and no residual chlorine remains. It typically consists of several stages:

1. Initial Chlorination: In this stage, chlorine is added to water, reacting with ammonia, organic matter
, and other impurities. This forms combined chlorine compounds.

2. Breakpoint: As chlorine continues to be added, it reacts with the remaining ammonia and
organic substances until the breakpoint is reached. At this point, all combined chlorine is
converted into free available chlorine (hypochlorous acid and hypochlorite ions).

3. Post-Breakpoint Chlorination: After the breakpoint, any additional chlorine
added contributes to the disinfection process, as free chlorine is an effective disinfectant
that kills bacteria, viruses, and pathogens. Application in Water Treatment:
 
- Disinfection: The primary application of breakpoint chlorination is to ensure the 
disinfection of water by eliminating harmful microorganisms, making it safe for consumption.

- Removal of Odor and Taste: It helps in reducing unpleasant tastes and odors caused by organic compounds and algae.

- Oxidation of Contaminants: Breakpoint chlorination oxidizes a wide range of contaminants, including ammonia, hydrogen sulfide, and iron, which can be subsequently removed through filtration.

- Residual Protection: Maintaining a chlorine residual post-breakpoint ensures continued protection against microbial regrowth as water travels through distribution systems. Overall, breakpoint chlorination is a vital step in ensuring the safety, quality, and palatability of drinking water in the water treatment process. 


### WATER TREATMENT MEthods

1. Chlorination:

• Strengths: Chlorination is one of the most widely used and cost-effective methods for
disinfecting water. It effectively kills most bacteria, viruses, and protozoa, making it suitable for large-scale municipal systems.

• Limitations: It may not remove chemical pollutants, heavy metals, or protozoa like Cryptosporidium. Chlorine residues can also lead to the formation of harmful disinfection by- products (DBPs), such as trihalomethanes (THMs).

2. Reverse Osmosis (RO):

oStrengths: RO is effective in removing dissolved salts, heavy metals (like arsenic, fluoride,
and lead), and other contaminants like pesticides and bacteria. It is suitable for areas with high salinity or high contamination of specific pollutants.

o Limitations: RO systems are expensive to set up and operate, requiring regular maintenance. It produces a significant amount of wastewater (brine) and may remove beneficial minerals, affecting water taste and nutritional quality.

3. Filtration (Sand/Activated Carbon):

• Strengths: Sand and activated carbon filters are efficient in removing suspended solids, sediments, chlorine, and some organic contaminants, improving water taste and clarity.

o Limitations: They are less effective at removing dissolved contaminants, heavy metals, or pathogens. Regular cleaning and maintenance are required to prevent clogging and bacterial growth.

4. Ultraviolet (UV) Treatment:

• Strengths: UV treatment effectively disinfects water by deactivating microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses, and protozoa. It does not produce harmful by-products.

• Limitations: UV light does not remove chemical contaminants, suspended solids, or heavy
metals. It requires clear water to be effective, as suspended particles can block UV rays.

5. Coagulation and Flocculation:

o Strengths: This method is effective at removing large particles, sediments, and
microorganisms by clumping them together for easier removal through sedimentation or filtration.

• Limitations: It is less effective in removing dissolved contamminants like heavy metals or
chemicals and requires proper chemical dosing and monitoring.






Water treatment is essential to convert raw water from natural sources into potable water that meets Indian
Standards (IS 10500) for safe drinking. The treatment process removes suspended solids, organic matter,
dissolved impurities, and pathogens. Among disinfection methods, chlorination plays a key role, with Break
Point Chlorination being superior to conventional chlorination for complete disinfection.

1. Flow Chart of Water Treatment Process
Raw Water → Screening → Aeration → Coagulation & Flocculation → Sedimentation → Filtration
Disinfection (Chlorination / Ozonation /UV) → Storage & Distribution


Step-wise Process

   1. Screening: Removal of large floating matter (leaves, debris, plastics).

   2. Aeration: Increases oxygen content, removes gases like CO2, H2S, and reduces iron & manganese.

   3. Coagulation & Flocculation: Addition of coagulants (e.g., alum) to destabilize colloids and form flocs.

   4. Sedimentation: Settling of suspended particles and flocs under gravity. 

   5. Filtration: Through sand or advanced filters to remove remaining turbidity, bacteria, and impurities.

   6. Disinfection: Kills pathogenic organisms. Chlorination is the most common method.

   7. Storage & Distribution: Treated water is stored in service reservoirs and supplied to consumers.

2. Conventional Chlorination

In conventional chlorination, a fixed dose of chlorine is added without considering chlorine demand of
water.

Often results in:
- Under-chlorination: Insufficient to kill all pathogens.

- Over-chlorination: Leaves residual chlorine causing bad taste and possible health risks.

Effectiveness is limited because chlorine first reacts with organic matter, ammonia, and other reducing
agents before acting on microorganisms.


3. Break Point Chlorination

Definition: Break Point Chlorination is the process of adding chlorine continuously until the chlorine
demand of the water is satisfied and a free chlorine residual is achieved.

Stages:
1. Initial Stage: Chlorine consumed in oxidizing organic matter, Fe2*, Mn2*, and reducing substances.

2. Combined Chlorine Formation: Chlorine reacts with ammonia to form chloramines (less
effective disinfectants).

3. Break Point: With further addition, chloramines are destroyed, and chlorine demand is
satisfied.

4. Free Chlorine Residual: Any additional chlorine remains as free chlorine, ensuring long-lasting
disinfection in the distribution system.

Graphical Representation: A chlorine dose vs residual chlorine curve shows the point beyond which
free chlorine rises steeply→this is the breakpoint.


4. Significance of Break Point Chlorination

Compared to Conventional Chlorination:

- Ensures destruction of pathogens more effectively.

- o Removes taste and odor-producing compounds.

- o Provides free residual chlorine (0.2-0.5 mg/L) as per IS 10500 for pipeline safety.

- o Prevents formation of excessive chloramines which have poor disinfecting power.

Public Health Importance:

o Protects against waterborne diseases (cholera, typhoid, dysentery).
o Maintains safety during storage and distribution.
Reduces risks of recontamination.
