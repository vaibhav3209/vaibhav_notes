    2. Why safe water supply is essential?

    Ans.

    Safe water supply is essential for the following reasons:

    1. Public Health Protection - Prevents the spread of water-borne diseases like cholera, typhoid, dysentery, and hepatitis.

    2. Hygiene and Sanitation - Provides clean water for bathing, washing, and sanitation, reducing infection risks.

    3. Food Safety - Ensures safe cooking, cleaning of utensils, and food processing.

    4. Industrial and Agricultural Needs - Supplies clean water required for industries, irrigation, and livestock, ensuring product quality and food security.

    5. Economic Development - Healthy communities with reliable water access are more productive and contribute to economic growth.

    6. Environmental Sustainability - Proper supply and management of safe water maintain ecological balance and groundwater recharge.

    7. Social Well-being - Availability of safe water improves quality of life and reduces the burden on healthcare systems.

    8. Resilience to Climate Change - Safe water systems help communities adapt to droughts, floods, and water scarcity.

    An efficient distribution system for safe water supply necessitates following key requirements:

    1. It should maintain consistent water pressure to ensure adequate flow to consumers.

    2. It must be well-maintained to prevent leaks and contamination risks.

    3. Regular monitoring and prompt repairs are essential to uphold water quality and service reliability.

    3. What is Blue baby syndrome?

    Ans.

    Blue Baby Syndrome is a condition in infants in which the skin turns bluish (cyanosis) due to insufficient oxygen in the blood. It is mainly caused by high nitrate levels in drinking water, which convert hemoglobin into methemoglobin, reducing its ability to carry oxygen. This leads to breathing difficulties, weakness, and in severe cases, can be life-threatening. The condition, also known as methemoglobinemia, can be prevented by ensuring safe drinking water with nitrate concentrations below the permissible limit of 45 mg/L (IS 10500 standard).

    5. What is water sanitation?

    Ans.

    Water sanitation refers to the process of ensuring that water is clean, safe, and free from harmful contaminants to prevent the spread of waterborne diseases. It involves the treatment, purification, and proper management of water to make it suitable for drinking, cooking, and other essential uses. Water sanitation practices include filtering, disinfecting, and monitoring water quality to remove impurities such as bacteria, viruses, and pollutants. Effective water sanitation is crucial for maintaining public health, reducing the risk of diseases like cholera, dysentery, and typhoid, and ensuring access to clean water for communities.

    Sanitation refers to the provision of facilities and practices that ensure the safe disposal of human waste, access to clean drinking water, and hygiene promotion.

    Its significance in public health cannot be overstated. Proper sanitation prevents the spread of waterborne diseases. It reduces the burden of diarrheal illnesses, and safeguards community health. It also contributes to environmental protection by preventing water pollution. Inadequate sanitation, conversely, poses significant health risks, particularly in densely populated areas, making it a critical aspect of public health infrastructure.

    9. Write down the difference between infiltration and percolation.

    Ans.

    Infiltration and percolation BOTH processes describing the movement of water through soil.

    Infiltration

    • Infiltration is the (process by which) or (Downward movement) water on the ground surface enters the soil or subsurface.
    • Occurs faster
    • Occurs on the surface and subsurface of the soil and limited to the upper soil layers, usually within a few feet of the surface.

    • Can be measured by infiltrometer

    • Infiltration primarily replenishes the upper soil moisture and helps support vegetation and shallow groundwater.

    • Infiltration rates are generally higher, as water initially enters the soil or sediments.
    • Water quality may improve during infiltration, as contaminants are filtered by the upper soil layers.
    • Infiltration rate is a measure of the rate at which a particular soil is able to absorb water.
    • It is measured in inches per hour or millimeters per hour with an instrument called infiltrometer.
    • The maximum amount of rainwater that can enter a soil in a given time is called the soil's infiltration capacity. When this maximum is reached, the soil becomes saturated.

    Percolation

    • Percolation is the movement of water through the soil and underlying layers, deeper into the ground.
    • The slow movement of water through the pores in soil or permeable rock
    • Occurs Slower
    • Can be measured by Lysimeter
    • Replenishes underground aquifers and the long-term storage of groundwater.

    • Percolation occurs after infiltration, as water continues to move deeper into the subsurface layers.

    • Percolation rates are typically slower, as water moves through denser subsurface layers.

    • Water quality improvements continue during percolation, with further natural filtration processes occurring.

    10. Infiltration Galleries

    An infiltration gallery is a- horizontal or nearly horizontal tunnel which is constructed through water bearing strata. It is sometimes referred to as horizontal well. The gallery is usually constructed of brick walls with slab roof as shown in the figure.

    The gallery obtains its water from water bearing strata by various porous drainpipes. These pipes are covered with gravel, pebble, etc. to prevent the entry of very fine material into the pipe. The gallery is laid at a slope and the water collected in the gallery is led to a sump from where it is pumped and supplicd to consumers after proper treatment. The manholes are provided along the infiltration gallery for the purposes of cleaning and inspection. The infiltration galleries are useful as sources of water supply when ground water is available in sufficient quantity just below ground level or so. The galleries are usually constructed at depth of about 5 to 10 meters from the ground level.

    Infiltration galleries and wells are important components of groundwater management systems, playing a crucial role in meeting the demand for safe underground water.

    • Infiltration galleries are underground structures designed to capture and store surface water runoff or treated wastewater, allowing it to slowly percolate into the ground.
    • They are typically constructed with perforated pipes or trenches filled with gravel, sand, or other porous materials to facilitate water infiltration.
    • Infiltration galleries help recharge aquifers by replenishing groundwater supplies, maintaining water levels, and improving water quality through natural filtration.
    • These structures are particularly useful in areas with limited surface water resources, aiding in drought mitigation and sustaining water availability.

    11. Artesian Spring:

    When ground water appears at the surface for any reason, springs are formed. They scrve as source of water supply for small towns, especially near hills or bases of hills. Some springs discharge hot water due to presence of sulphur and other minerals in their formations. These hot springs cannot be used to supply water for domestic purposes. But hot water is found to cure some of the human disorders. A good spring proves to be a sure source of water. But it is difficult to find a good spring for the purpose of water supply scheme. Artesian spring is a source of ground water that is forced to the earth's surface by of pressure caused by underground layers of rock surrounding the aquifer. It differs from a regular water well in that it doesn't require a pump to get the water to the surface. The only requirement is gravity and the pressure it creates. For this reason, Artesian springs occur at the base of a mountain or hill where the water can flow down into a well.

    Artesian springs form when groundwater trapped under pressure within an aquifer naturally flows to the surface. Water from rain or snow seeps into the ground in elevated areas, percolating through porous rock and becoming confined between impermeable layers. As more water enters the aquifer, hydrostatic pressure builds due to gravity and the trapped nature of the water. When this pressure becomes strong enough, and a fissure or crack provides an outlet, the water is forced upward to the surface, creating an artesian spring. This natural process requires no pumping, as the water flows freely under the pressure.

    12. Wells:

    Wells are vertical or slanting excavations drilled or dug into the ground to access underground aquifers or reservoirs of groundwater. They are equipped with pumps to extract water, and their depth depends on the depth of the water table or the specific aquifer being tapped.

    Wells provide a direct source of safe groundwater for various purposes, including drinking, irrigation, and industrial processes. Properly constructed and maintained wells can yield a sustainable and reliable source of clean water.

    13. Water Standards

    Ans.

    Drinking water standards are guidelines set to ensure that water is safe for consumption, free from contaminants that could harm human health. These standards vary across countries but generally include limits on various physical, chemical, and biological parameters. - Physical Standards: These focus on aspects like color, turbidity (cloudiness), odor, and taste. Water should be clear and free of visible particles, unpleasant smells, and tastes that indicate contamination. - Chemical Standards: These measure the presence of substances such as heavy metals (lead, arsenic), pesticides, and minerals (fluoride, chlorine). Excessive levels of these chemicals can pose serious health risks, such as cancer or kidney damage. - Biological Standards: These address the presence of harmful microorganisms like bacteria, viruses, and protozoa. High concentrations of pathogens can cause waterborne diseases like cholera and typhoid. Safe drinking water should have no detectable pathogens. - Microbiological Standards: Water should be free from harmful microorganisms such as Escherichia coli (Е. coli) and coliform bacteria, which indicate fecal contamination.

    14. Water Supply Requirements

    Ans.

    Urban Water Supply: Urban systems must cater to large populations, requiring extensive infrastructure like reservoirs, treatment plants, and pipelines. The water source must be reliable, often drawn from rivers, lakes, or groundwater. Urban systems also need advanced treatment facilities to ensure water quality, removing pollutants like industrial waste, chemicals, and pathogens.

    Rural Water Supply: In rural areas, water sources are typically more decentralized, such as wells, boreholes, or small rivers. Rural systems often rely on simpler infrastructure, but they face challenges in maintaining reliable water quality due to limited access to advanced treatment technologies. Water sources may also be more prone to contamination from agriculture or animal waste.

    The estimation of water demand for a developing urban area is an important step in water supply engineering, as it helps in designing systems that can meet both present and future needs.

    1. Factors Affecting Water Requirement:
    2. Population size and growth: The demand increases with population; design periods of 15-30 years are generally adopted.
    3. Per Capita Requirement (LPCD): According to IS 1172:1993, the water demand in Indian cities varies from 100-150 liters per capita per day (LPCD) depending on living standards and service levels.
    4. Climate: Hot and dry regions show higher water demand compared to cooler areas.
    5. Standard of living: Metropolitan cities with modern facilities need more water compared to small towns.
    6. Industrial and commercial activities: Contribute about 15-20% of total demand in urban areas.

    7. Losses and wastage: Unaccounted-for water and leakage usually range between 15-20% of total supply.

    8. Estimation of Demand: The total water demand is calculated in terms of:

    9. Average daily demand = Population LPCD
    10. Maximum daily demand ≈ 1.8 × Average demand

    11. Peak hourly demand ≈2.7 × Average demand This ensures that the system can handle both normal and peak conditions.

    12. Sources of Water to Meet Demand:

    13. Surface Water: Rivers, lakes, and reservoirs are the primary sources, though they require full treatment (sedimentation, filtration, disinfection).
    14. Groundwater: Wells, tube wells, and springs provide relatively clean water but may contain hardness, fluoride, or iron beyond limits prescribed in IS 10500:2012 (Drinking Water Standards).
    15. Rainwater Harvesting: Useful for groundwater recharge and reducing demand on conventional sources.

    16. Other Sources: Desalination (in coastal areas) and reclaimed wastewater for non-potable use.

    17. Importance in Environmental Engineering: By estimating water demand and selecting suitable sources, engineers ensure safe, adequate, and sustainable supply. This protects public health, supports industries and agriculture, and promotes environmental.

    16. Analyze the key physical, chemical, and bacteriological parameters of drinking water quality and

    evaluate their compliance with Indian Standards (IS 10500).

    Ans.

    The quality of drinking water plays a vital role in protecting human health. Contaminated water can cause serious health hazards, ranging from aesthetic issues to life-threatening diseases. Therefore, water quality is assessed based on physical, chemical, and bacteriological parameters. In India, the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) - IS 10500:2012 (2nd Revision, reaffirmed 2017) specifies the acceptable and permissible limits for drinking water to ensure safety, potability, and consumer acceptability.

    1. Physical Parameters

    These parameters affect the appearance, taste, and acceptability of water, though some also have indirect health impacts.

    • Color:

      • o Limit: 5 Hazen units (permissible up to 15).

      • o Significance: Color arises from decayed organic matter, iron, or industrial waste. Excessive color makes water unacceptable.

    • Turbidity:

      • o Limit: 1 NTU (permissible up to 5 NTU).

      • • Significance: Turbid water interferes with disinfection, affects aesthetics, and may harbor microorganisms.

    • Taste & Odor:

      • Must be agreeable and free from offensive smell.

      • Causes: Algae, organic pollution, or chlorination byproducts.

    • Total Dissolved Solids (TDS):

      • Desirable: 500 mg/L (permissible 2000 mg/L).

      • Significance: High TDS affects taste, causes scaling, and in very high concentrations, may affect health.

    Analysis: Physical parameters ensure consumer acceptability and indicate possible pollution sources. Excess values require treatment like sedimentation, coagulation, and filtration.

    1. Chemical Parameters These determine the suitability for long-term human consumption and detect toxic or harmful substances.

    2. pH Value:

      • Acceptable: 6.5 -8.5.

      • Too low → corrosive water; too high → encrustation of pipes. - Hardness (as CaCO3):

      • Desirable: 200 mg/L; permissible 600 mg/L. Excess hardness causes kidney stones and scaling of pipelines/boilers.

    3. Chlorides:

      o Limit: 250 mg/L (permissible 1000 mg/L).

      o High chlorides give salty taste and corrode pipes.

    4. Fluoride:

      • Optimum: 1.0 mg/L; Max: 1.5 mg/L.

      • Low levels (<0.5) → dental caries; high levels → dental and skeletal fluorosis.

    5. Nitrates (as NO3): Limit: 45 mg/L. •Higher concentrations cause Methemoglobinemia (Blue Baby Syndrome) in infants.

    6. Iron & Manganese: o Limit: 0.3 mg/L (Fe), 0.1 mg/L (Mn). Cause reddish-brown staining and unpleasant taste.

    7. Sulphates: Limit: 200 mg/L (permissible 400 mg/L). High sulphates cause a laxative effect.

    Analysis: Chemical parameters ensure water is free from harmful toxins and long-term health effects. Treatment like defluoridation, demineralization, and ion exchange is required when limits are exceeded.

    1. Bacteriological Parameters These are the most critical for public health, since microbial contamination leads to waterborne diseases.

    2. Total Coliform Bacteria: o Standard: 0 per 100 mL.

    3. Presence indicates possible contamination and unsanitary conditions.

    4. E. coli (Fecal Coliforms):

    Must be absent in 100 mL of sample.

    Directly indicates fecal contamination and presence of pathogens like those causing cholera, typhoid, dysentery, and hepatitis.

    Analysis: Even a single positive bacteriological sample means the water is unsafe. Chlorination, ozonation, or UV treatment are necessary for disinfection.

    BREAK POIINT Chlorination

    Break point chlorination is a critical process in water treatment that involves the addition of chlorine until the point at which all the available impurities are oxidized , and no residual chlorine remains. It typically consists of several stages:

    1. Initial Chlorination: In this stage, chlorine is added to water, reacting with ammonia, organic matter , and other impurities. This forms combined chlorine compounds.

    2. Breakpoint: As chlorine continues to be added, it reacts with the remaining ammonia and organic substances until the breakpoint is reached. At this point, all combined chlorine is converted into free available chlorine (hypochlorous acid and hypochlorite ions).

    3. Post-Breakpoint Chlorination: After the breakpoint, any additional chlorine added contributes to the disinfection process, as free chlorine is an effective disinfectant that kills bacteria, viruses, and pathogens. Application in Water Treatment:

    4. Disinfection: The primary application of breakpoint chlorination is to ensure the disinfection of water by eliminating harmful microorganisms, making it safe for consumption.

    5. Removal of Odor and Taste: It helps in reducing unpleasant tastes and odors caused by organic compounds and algae.

    6. Oxidation of Contaminants: Breakpoint chlorination oxidizes a wide range of contaminants, including ammonia, hydrogen sulfide, and iron, which can be subsequently removed through filtration.

    7. Residual Protection: Maintaining a chlorine residual post-breakpoint ensures continued protection against microbial regrowth as water travels through distribution systems. Overall, breakpoint chlorination is a vital step in ensuring the safety, quality, and palatability of drinking water in the water treatment process.

    WATER TREATMENT MEthods

    1. Chlorination:

    • Strengths: Chlorination is one of the most widely used and cost-effective methods for disinfecting water. It effectively kills most bacteria, viruses, and protozoa, making it suitable for large-scale municipal systems.

    • Limitations: It may not remove chemical pollutants, heavy metals, or protozoa like Cryptosporidium. Chlorine residues can also lead to the formation of harmful disinfection by- products (DBPs), such as trihalomethanes (THMs).

    1. Reverse Osmosis (RO):

    oStrengths: RO is effective in removing dissolved salts, heavy metals (like arsenic, fluoride, and lead), and other contaminants like pesticides and bacteria. It is suitable for areas with high salinity or high contamination of specific pollutants.

    o Limitations: RO systems are expensive to set up and operate, requiring regular maintenance. It produces a significant amount of wastewater (brine) and may remove beneficial minerals, affecting water taste and nutritional quality.

    1. Filtration (Sand/Activated Carbon):

    • Strengths: Sand and activated carbon filters are efficient in removing suspended solids, sediments, chlorine, and some organic contaminants, improving water taste and clarity.

    o Limitations: They are less effective at removing dissolved contaminants, heavy metals, or pathogens. Regular cleaning and maintenance are required to prevent clogging and bacterial growth.

    1. Ultraviolet (UV) Treatment:

    • Strengths: UV treatment effectively disinfects water by deactivating microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses, and protozoa. It does not produce harmful by-products.

    • Limitations: UV light does not remove chemical contaminants, suspended solids, or heavy metals. It requires clear water to be effective, as suspended particles can block UV rays.

    1. Coagulation and Flocculation:

    o Strengths: This method is effective at removing large particles, sediments, and microorganisms by clumping them together for easier removal through sedimentation or filtration.

    • Limitations: It is less effective in removing dissolved contamminants like heavy metals or chemicals and requires proper chemical dosing and monitoring.

    Water treatment is essential to convert raw water from natural sources into potable water that meets Indian Standards (IS 10500) for safe drinking. The treatment process removes suspended solids, organic matter, dissolved impurities, and pathogens. Among disinfection methods, chlorination plays a key role, with Break Point Chlorination being superior to conventional chlorination for complete disinfection.

    1. Flow Chart of Water Treatment Process Raw Water → Screening → Aeration → Coagulation & Flocculation → Sedimentation → Filtration Disinfection (Chlorination / Ozonation /UV) → Storage & Distribution

    Step-wise Process

    1. Screening: Removal of large floating matter (leaves, debris, plastics).

    2. Aeration: Increases oxygen content, removes gases like CO2, H2S, and reduces iron & manganese.

    3. Coagulation & Flocculation: Addition of coagulants (e.g., alum) to destabilize colloids and form flocs.

    4. Sedimentation: Settling of suspended particles and flocs under gravity.

    5. Filtration: Through sand or advanced filters to remove remaining turbidity, bacteria, and impurities.

    6. Disinfection: Kills pathogenic organisms. Chlorination is the most common method.

    7. Storage & Distribution: Treated water is stored in service reservoirs and supplied to consumers.

    8. Conventional Chlorination

    In conventional chlorination, a fixed dose of chlorine is added without considering chlorine demand of water.

    Often results in: - Under-chlorination: Insufficient to kill all pathogens.

    • Over-chlorination: Leaves residual chlorine causing bad taste and possible health risks.

    Effectiveness is limited because chlorine first reacts with organic matter, ammonia, and other reducing agents before acting on microorganisms.

    1. Break Point Chlorination

    Definition: Break Point Chlorination is the process of adding chlorine continuously until the chlorine demand of the water is satisfied and a free chlorine residual is achieved.

    Stages: 1. Initial Stage: Chlorine consumed in oxidizing organic matter, Fe2, Mn2, and reducing substances.

    1. Combined Chlorine Formation: Chlorine reacts with ammonia to form chloramines (less effective disinfectants).

    2. Break Point: With further addition, chloramines are destroyed, and chlorine demand is satisfied.

    3. Free Chlorine Residual: Any additional chlorine remains as free chlorine, ensuring long-lasting disinfection in the distribution system.

    Graphical Representation: A chlorine dose vs residual chlorine curve shows the point beyond which free chlorine rises steeply→this is the breakpoint.

    1. Significance of Break Point Chlorination

    Compared to Conventional Chlorination:

    • Ensures destruction of pathogens more effectively.

    • o Removes taste and odor-producing compounds.

    • o Provides free residual chlorine (0.2-0.5 mg/L) as per IS 10500 for pipeline safety.

    • o Prevents formation of excessive chloramines which have poor disinfecting power.

    Public Health Importance:

    o Protects against waterborne diseases (cholera, typhoid, dysentery). o Maintains safety during storage and distribution. Reduces risks of recontamination.
